\begin{abstract}
\noindent This brief exists because you asked a question I could not answer properly in
conversation: \emph{a credential can be stored locally, but how does that let you verify
the agent, tell it apart from a bad one, and call it trusted?}

Section~\ref{sec:verify} is the honest answer, and it is more interesting than I first
thought, because the answer is partly negative. Sections~\ref{sec:bg}
and~\ref{sec:proto} are the payments background needed to make that answer meaningful:
no prior payments knowledge is assumed, and I have tried to describe each mechanism in
terms of what it actually guarantees rather than what its marketing says.
Section~\ref{sec:crypto} is where I think your area and this problem genuinely meet, and
it is the part I would most like your reaction to.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Delegated authorisation, agent attestation, trusted execution, secure computation,
private information retrieval, payment mandates, UPI, revocation.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{What the project is}

\IEEEPARstart{A}{n} AI agent embedded in a device---an appliance, a vehicle charger, a
diagnostic instrument---detects that it needs something and buys it. Nobody is watching. The owner
approved a spending arrangement weeks ago and is not present at the moment of payment.

The project builds the layer that decides whether such a payment should proceed, and
produces the evidence needed afterwards if it should not have.

\begin{keybox}
Every payment rail now shipping for agents enforces \emph{how much} an agent may spend.
None verifies \emph{what it actually bought}.
\end{keybox}

Concretely: a mandate authorising \rs 2{,}000 per purchase will happily authorise a
\rs 600 charge against a basket the owner approved at \rs 118. No limit is violated. No
rule is broken. The owner is overcharged by \rs 482 and the dispute system has no
category to file it under. I have this running in a simulator; it is two commands.

\subsection{Scope}
The work begins when the basket is complete and the agent requests payment, and ends
when the money has moved and the evidence is recorded. Product discovery, price
comparison and supplier choice are upstream and out of scope. Restricting to
\emph{repetitive} purchases---reordering the same consumable---is what makes advance
authorisation meaningful, because the owner can approve a pattern even though they
cannot approve each instance.

\section{Payments background}\label{sec:bg}

This section assumes nothing about payments. Skip it if any of it is familiar.

\subsection{UPI}
UPI (Unified Payments Interface) is India's real-time retail payment system, operated by
NPCI, a not-for-profit consortium of banks. A user is addressed by a \emph{virtual
payment address} (VPA) such as \texttt{name@bank}, which resolves to a bank account. A
payment is a message routed through a central switch that debits one bank and credits
another, settling in seconds.

Two properties matter here. First, UPI is \textbf{zero-MDR} for most merchant
transactions---there is no interchange fee, which removes the usual revenue model.
Second, authentication is by a 4- or 6-digit \emph{UPI PIN} entered on a device bound to
the user, and the security model assumes \textbf{a human is present}.

\subsection{Mandates}
A mandate is standing authorisation to debit an account without fresh authentication
each time. Two variants matter.

\textbf{UPI Autopay} is a recurring mandate bound to one payee, one amount ceiling, and
a schedule. It is built for subscriptions. It cannot express ``pay whichever supplier
turns out to be cheapest,'' because the payee is fixed at creation.

\textbf{UPI Reserve Pay}, formally \emph{Single Block Multiple Debits}, blocks funds in
the user's own account, and a merchant then debits against that blocked pool repeatedly
until it is exhausted or expires. Ceiling \rs 10{,}000, validity 90 days, and---this is
the constraint that shapes the architecture---\textbf{one active block per merchant per
customer}.

The security-relevant fact: \textbf{authentication happens once, at block creation.}
Every debit afterwards is unauthenticated by the human. The rail has no field
identifying who or what initiated a debit, so it cannot distinguish an agent from a
person.

\subsection{Cards, and why they differ}
Card networks have spent two decades building an adversarial infrastructure that UPI has
not needed: tokenisation, network-side authorisation policy, and a formal dispute
process with defined liability shifts and a 120-day chargeback window.

\emph{Tokenisation} replaces a card number with a network-issued token scoped to a
context---this is what Apple Pay does. The token is a reference, not a bearer
instrument: it holds no value and de-tokenises inside the network to the real account.

\subsection{The asymmetry that defines the architecture}
UPI is cheap, ubiquitous in India, and has no notion of an agent. Cards are expensive,
have network-enforced scoping, carry an agent identifier, and possess a dispute process.

The system therefore spans both, and the owner chooses which funds a given agent.

\section{The protocol landscape}\label{sec:proto}

Four systems shipped in the last eighteen months. I describe each by what it
cryptographically guarantees.

\subsection{AP2 (Google, September 2025)}
Defines three signed artifacts as W3C Verifiable Credentials: an \emph{Intent Mandate}
(what the user asked for), a \emph{Cart Mandate} (what the agent selected), and a
\emph{Payment Mandate}. Guarantees non-repudiation of each artifact. Notably, it defines
the envelope but \textbf{not a constraint language for what goes inside it}, so
``does the cart satisfy the intent'' is left unspecified.

\subsection{Mastercard Agent Pay and Verifiable Intent}
\emph{Agentic Tokens} extend card tokenisation with an agent identifier and a scope
object---spend caps, merchant categories, expiry---validated \textbf{network-side at
every authorisation}. \emph{Verifiable Intent}, open-sourced March 2026, is a credential
travelling with the authorisation that cryptographically links the transaction to the
human mandate behind it.

Mastercard's framing of the agent problem is called \emph{Know Your Agent}, and it asks
three questions that map exactly onto yours: is the controlling human verified, are the
agent's keys in TEE hardware, and is the delegation scope well defined.

\subsection{P3P (Pine Labs, June 2026)}
The closest system to this project, and live in production. Runs agentic payments over
UPI Reserve Pay using HTTP 402, with an identity and delegation layer called
\emph{Grantex} supplying agent identity, spend limits and an audit trail.

\q{Its public documentation stops at a quickstart and does not describe any binding
between a payment and an approved order. If such a binding exists, a substantial part of
this project's contribution is already taken. Resolving this is the highest-priority
open item.}

\subsection{x402 and MPP}
Both revive HTTP status code 402. x402 (Coinbase) settles USDC on-chain via EIP-3009
gasless authorisations verified by a facilitator. MPP (Stripe and Tempo) is a general
HTTP authentication scheme, on an IETF track, with payment methods defined as separate
specifications.

Both solve \emph{paying for an HTTP resource}, where the thing purchased \emph{is} the
request. Neither needs an order record, because there is no basket. They are adjacent,
not competing.

\section{Agent verification: the answer to your question}\label{sec:verify}

You observed that a credential can be stored locally, and asked how that lets us verify
the agent, distinguish it from a bad agent, and treat it as trusted.

The short answer is that a local credential gives \textbf{one} of the four properties we
need, and the fourth is not obtainable by cryptographic means at all. Separating them is
most of the work.

\subsection{Four distinct properties}

\paragraph{1. Authentication --- which key signed this?}
An Ed25519 keypair held by the agent, signing each request. Establishes that
\emph{something holding that key} produced the message.

This is what a locally stored credential gives you, and it is genuinely necessary. It is
also the weakest of the four. Against an adversary with host access it establishes
nothing, because a software key can be read out of memory and used elsewhere. An agent
authenticated this way is indistinguishable from an attacker who copied its key.

\paragraph{2. Binding --- is the key where we think it is?}
Generate and hold the key in a hardware-isolated environment: a TEE (ARM TrustZone,
Intel TDX, AMD SEV-SNP), a Secure Enclave, or a TPM. The private key never leaves; the
environment performs signatures on request.

This upgrades the claim from \emph{something has the key} to \emph{something on this
specific device has the key}. Key extraction now requires defeating the hardware. This
is what Mastercard's KYA framework is asking about when it asks whether agent keys are
in TEE hardware.

\paragraph{3. Attestation --- what code is running?}
The interesting one. A TEE can produce a signed \emph{quote} containing a measurement
(a hash) of the code loaded into it, signed by a key rooted in the hardware
manufacturer's PKI. A remote verifier checks the signature chain and compares the
measurement against a known-good value.

This gives what authentication cannot: evidence that \emph{this specific software build}
is running, unmodified, in genuine hardware. It is the mechanism that separates a
legitimate agent from a modified one.

It requires a \textbf{registry}: a measurement is only meaningful if someone publishes
that measurement $m$ corresponds to approved agent build $B$. This is precisely what
NPCI's proposed Unified Agent Protocol and Mastercard's KYA are reaching for, and
neither has shipped a specification.

\paragraph{4. Correctness --- did the agent do the right thing?}
Here the stack stops helping, and this is the part I find genuinely interesting.

\begin{keybox}
Attestation of an AI agent certifies strictly less than attestation of a deterministic
program, because the same attested binary produces different behaviour depending on
input the attestation does not cover.
\end{keybox}

An attested, unmodified, correctly-keyed agent reads a supplier's product description
which contains adversarial text, and buys the wrong thing. Every cryptographic property
above holds. The measurement is correct. The signature verifies. The hardware is genuine.
The purchase is wrong.

Attestation covers the code and, at best, the model weights. It does not and cannot
cover the inference, because the inference depends on runtime input from an untrusted
source. For a conventional program we would call that input validation and push it
inside the measured boundary. For an agent whose entire purpose is to consume untrusted
text and act on it, that move is unavailable.

\subsection{The consequence for the architecture}
Because property 4 is unreachable by attestation, it has to be obtained structurally:
\textbf{constrain what the agent is permitted to do, so that being wrong is not
sufficient to cause loss.}

This is the project's central mechanism. The owner signs a constraint in advance. Before
settlement, a deterministic check tests whether the requested payment satisfies that
constraint. A compromised agent can still request anything; it cannot obtain settlement
of a request that fails the check.

Two design rules follow, both non-obvious enough that I got them wrong first:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Enforcement must sit outside the agent's trust boundary.} If the limit is
checked by the agent, an injected agent moves the limit. It is checked by a party the
agent cannot influence.
\item \textbf{The check must be deterministic.} No model participates in the approval
path. An issuer cannot decline a payment on a probability---it needs a reason that can
be stated in a dispute. This also means the check itself cannot be attacked by the same
class of adversary.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{An empirical result that surprised me}
I implemented the order as a signed artifact and tested three signing arrangements:
signed by the agent, by the merchant, and by both.

\textbf{All three approve a self-consistent inflated order.} If the line items genuinely
sum to \rs 600 and the signature verifies, nothing in the system objects---because the
order is the only surviving record of what was agreed, so there is nothing to compare it
against. What refuses it is a per-payment ceiling close to the expected basket size.

The lesson generalises, and it is the same lesson as your question one layer up:
\textbf{a signature establishes authorship, not truth.} I found this useful discipline
for thinking about the rest of the design.

\section{Where I think your work and this problem meet}\label{sec:crypto}

Sections~\ref{sec:bg}--\ref{sec:verify} are mostly systems engineering. This section is
what I think is actually research, and all of it is closer to your area than mine.

\subsection{Problem A --- the evidence trail is a surveillance database}
To settle disputes, the system records what every machine bought, bound to an owner
identity, indefinitely. That is a comprehensive record of a household's or a firm's
consumption, held by an operator with a commercial interest in it.

For a dispute we need to reveal \textbf{one purchase to one arbiter}. Everything else
should remain hidden---from the arbiter, from the merchant, and ideally from the
operator holding the log.

\q{Can the dispute evidence be constructed so that the owner can prove a specific
payment violated a specific mandate, revealing neither the mandate's other terms nor any
other purchase, and without the operator holding a readable history?}

This looks like selective disclosure over a commitment---a Merkle-committed log with
zero-knowledge membership and predicate proofs, or anonymous-credential techniques
applied to the mandate. I do not know which is right, or whether the verification cost
is compatible with an authorisation path that must complete in well under a second.

\subsection{Problem B --- checking a mandate without learning it}
Today the operator sees both the mandate and the order, because it must evaluate one
against the other. That is a large amount of trust placed in a party that has no
business knowing what a household buys.

The check itself is arithmetic and narrow: a comparison against an amount ceiling,
membership in a merchant set, membership in a category set, a period-budget subtraction,
and a validity window. These are exactly the primitives secure computation handles well.

\q{Can the order check run as a two-party computation between the agent's host and the
verifier, so that the verifier learns only the boolean verdict and neither party learns
the other's input?}

The merchant allow-list is a private set membership query, which is where I suspect your
distributed point function work is directly applicable. The realistic obstacle is
latency: an authorisation decision has a budget of roughly 150\,ms end to end, and I do
not know what is achievable in that envelope for a set of realistic size.

\subsection{Problem C --- revocation}
An owner revokes an agent. Revocation must reach two rails and any funds already in
flight, and---critically---a verifier must be able to establish \emph{at authorisation
time} that a credential is still valid.

Naive approaches leak: a status query reveals which agent is transacting and when. This
is the classic tension between revocation freshness, verification cost, and privacy,
which accumulators and short-lived credentials attack from different directions.

\q{What revocation construction gives sub-second freshness without the status check
itself becoming a tracking channel?}

There is also a timing question with real liability attached: if the owner revokes at
$t$ and a payment authorises at $t+\epsilon$, who bears the loss? The system currently
produces a signed, timestamped revocation record so the question is at least decidable,
but the correct construction is not obvious to me.

\subsection{Problem D --- metadata}
Even with contents hidden, the pattern leaks: purchase frequency, timing, amount
distribution, and the identity of the settling merchant. For a household this reveals
occupancy, travel and health. This is your listed interest in metadata protection,
applied to a setting where the observer is a regulated financial intermediary that
cannot simply be removed.

\subsection{Honest assessment of the split}
The order check, the two-rail architecture, the reconciliation logic and the control
surface are engineering. They are substantial and they work, but they are not research
and I would not claim otherwise.

Problems A through D are research, and I cannot do them alone. That is the reason for
asking you.

\section{Threat model}

\subsection{Assumed capabilities}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The agent may be compromised at runtime} by adversarial input. This is
assumed, not defended against. Prompt injection is unsolved and the design does not
claim to prevent it.
\item \textbf{The merchant is adversarial.} It controls all product data reaching the
agent and has a direct financial incentive to influence selection.
\item \textbf{The operator is honest-but-curious.} It follows the protocol and would
like to learn what everyone buys. Problems A and B exist to reduce what it can learn.
\item \textbf{Rails are trusted for correctness}, not for privacy.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Security goals}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item A compromised agent cannot obtain settlement of a payment violating the owner's
signed constraint.
\item Every settled payment yields evidence sufficient to adjudicate a dispute.
\item Revocation is effective across both rails and any funds in flight.
\item \emph{(Target, not achieved)} No party learns more about the owner's purchasing
than its role requires.
\end{enumerate}

Goal 4 is the research contribution. Goals 1--3 are built and tested.

\section{What exists today}

A simulator with UPI-shaped rails: a double-entry ledger where postings must sum to
zero, a two-leg payment with a suspense account between the legs, injectable failures, a
reconciliation sweep, Ed25519-signed authority and order records, the deterministic
order check, cross-rail virtual-card settlement, revocation, and an owner console. 69
tests, no network dependency.

Four results the implementation produced, each asserted by a test rather than argued:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item Signatures on an order do not prevent overcharging, under any signer arrangement.
\item Routing funds through an agent-controlled card destroys merchant scoping on the
funding leg, because the mandate can then only name the card.
\item A failed-and-refunded purchase permanently consumes the owner's periodic budget
unless headroom is explicitly released on reversal.
\item Revocation is three operations; implementations typically perform one.
\end{enumerate}

Not answers---you were clear that you would not have them, and the interesting parts do
not have known answers.

What would help most: a judgement on whether Problems A through D are the right

After that, whichever of the four you find most interesting is the one I would rather
