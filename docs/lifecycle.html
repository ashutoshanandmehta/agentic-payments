Architecture

The payment lifecycle

One purchase, from the agent finishing a basket to the evidence being recorded. Two rails, two legs, and one window where the money is neither the owner's nor the merchant's.

States

The exposure window

FUNDED through PAID is the only interval where the money is neither the owner's nor the merchant's. Every risk this architecture adds lives inside it, and every mitigation is about making it shorter — load the exact basket total, pay immediately, sweep on failure.

Three rules the lifecycle enforces

1. The check runs before any money moves

CHECKING sits between ORDER SIGNED and FUNDING, not after. A payment that fails the order check never reaches a rail, so a refusal costs nothing and leaves nothing to undo.

2. Never refund on a timeout

A timeout means the outcome is genuinely unknown — the merchant may have been paid. Refunding then pays twice. RECONCILING is a distinct state precisely so that "we do not know" cannot be silently collapsed into "it failed."

The sweep asks both rails what actually posted. Both posted, settle. Debit only, reverse. Neither, refuse.

3. Refunds release headroom

REFUNDING returns the money and the mandate headroom the funding leg consumed. Without the second half, a run of failures silently exhausts the owner's cap while their balance looks correct.

Revocation cuts across everything

REVOKED is reachable from any live state and is three actions, not one: kill the card token, revoke the UPI block, sweep any resting float. Each is reported separately, because a revoke that half worked is worse than one that failed loudly.

The hard case is revocation during FUNDED. The block is dead but the money is already on the card and perfectly spendable — which is why the sweep is part of revoking rather than a follow-up.

Two layers, not one

These are purchase states. Each rail leg internally runs its own transaction state machine — created, authorised, debit pending, debited, credit pending, credited, settled, with declined, reversed and timed-out branches.

One purchase therefore spans two transactions. The cross-rail receipt exists because nothing else records that they were the same purchase.
